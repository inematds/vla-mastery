📈 Size and projections: humanoids and AI robotics

Projections vary enormously because the market is still taking shape — and are not comparable with each other: some measure only humanoid hardware in 2035, others add software and services in 2050. That is why this module prefers 2026 facts (rounds, IPOs, shipments) and always cites each projection with the bank and year. "AI robotics" market figures with no author and no defined scope (like those circulating in 2025–2026) are left out.

📊 Read projections with healthy skepticism

  • Goldman Sachs — previously: US$ 38 bn in humanoids by 2035; a review cited in the press mentions ~US$ 138 bn in 2035, not confirmed in the original report.
  • Morgan Stanley (Apr/2025) — ~US$ 4.7 trillion in humanoid revenue in 2050, ~1 billion units (according to CNBC). The "US$ 7.5 trillion" circulating in 2026 has no primary source.
  • Citi / UBS — up to ~US$ 7 trillion by 2050 (Citi) and US$ 1.4–1.7 trillion (UBS), according to the press.
  • 2026 fact — China accounted for ~97% of global humanoid shipments in 1H/2026, according to Poder360.

🔗 Value chain: chips, actuators, models, data

The value chain has layers: silicon (NVIDIA, edge), actuators and hands, foundation models, integrators e data. As with any platform, margin tends to concentrate where there is defensible differentiation — and the dominant thesis in 2026 is that operational data e models form the moat, while hardware tends to commoditize. A counterpoint with numbers: Unitree, which sells hardware, went public at ~US$ 9 bn (Aug/2026), and in 2025 its humanoid revenue (¥ 867.8 mn) already surpassed that of quadrupeds (CNBC, citing the prospectus).

💡 Where the moat is

Humanoid hardware is getting cheaper (Unitree showed this). What isn't easily commoditized is data flywheel: whoever has robots in operation collects data that improves the model, which sells more robots. The data/model layer is the one that most resembles software in margin. In 2026 the data source also changed: egocentric human video became the main pre-training input (Figure's Index dataset, Aug/2026; "20,000+ hours of human egocentric video" in GR00T N1.7, Apr/2026), which reduces the dependence on an in-house fleet alone.

🏷️ Business models: RaaS, licensing, data

How do you monetize? Three models dominate. RaaS (robot-as-a-service) charges by the hour/task, removing the client's CapEx and aligning revenue with the value delivered. Model licensing sells the brain to hardware OEMs. And data monetization turns operation into an asset that feeds back into the model. 2026 examples: the Pi API (beta, Oct 13, 2026) from Physical Intelligence sells data upload, fine-tuning, and hosted inference via API, without saying which model is behind it; the Gemini Robotics-ER 2 ships through Google AI Studio (Jul/2026); and the GR00T N1.7 has open weights under the NVIDIA Open Model License, with commercial use (Apr/2026). Open × non-commercial × API × early access has become a business decision, not just a technical one.

💼 Where the jobs/skills are

The sector needs profiles that this track maps out. Research in VLA (architectures, training), Robotics MLOps (deploy, fleet monitoring), integration (ROS2, hardware, safety) and teleoperation data engineering (data collection pipeline) are the hottest areas. The competitive advantage lies in combining ML with physical engineering. In Brazil (according to the press): the country installed ~4,300 new industrial robots in 2025, +38% (Poder360 citing the IFR); SENAI SC has CTRob, an advanced robotics and AI center with R$ 60 million+ from Petrobras; SENAI MS bought 2 humanoids for R&D (2026); SENAI CIMATEC offers a specialization in autonomous systems; and Mercado Libre is the first major Latin American company with a humanoid (Digit) in operation, still in the US. As of Oct 2026, no Latin American VLA startup with a publicly identified funding round exists.

⚠️ Risks: hype cycle, sim-to-real gap, regulation

Reading risks is as strategic as reading opportunity. The sector may enter the trough of disillusionment of the hype cycle if viral demos don't convert into profitable deployments. The residual sim-to-real gap, immature regulation, and the adoption timing are the main brakes. Anyone investing or building a career needs to model the pessimistic scenario. A concrete 2026 signal: with ~150 companies making humanoids in China, an NDRC representative publicly admitted the risk of overcapacity and a bubble (per MERICS and Carnegie, Apr/2026).

⚡ The lesson of the hype cycle

Every transformative technology goes through a peak of expectations, a trough of disillusionment, and only then a plateau of productivity. Many people judge that generalist robotics is near the peak in 2026 (that's a reading, not a measurement). The winners will be whoever survives the trough — usually those with real revenue, not just demos.

📊 The 4 risks to model

  • Hype cycle — delivery expectations pulled forward; valuation correction.
  • Residual sim-to-real gap — the last 10% of reliability is the most expensive.
  • Regulation — still immature: the humanoid standard (ISO 25785-1) is only a draft and the European AI Act was postponed by the Digital Omnibus (Jul/2026); see M3.6.
  • Timing — Enter too early and you burn cash; too late and you miss the window.
