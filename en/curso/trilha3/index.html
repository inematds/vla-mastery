3.1

🌉 Sim-to-Real: Domain Randomization and Transfer

Train on millions of simulated episodes and run on the physical robot.

What it is: the systematic divergence between what the simulator models and what the physical world does — contact friction, actuator delay, camera noise, deformable objects.

Why it matters: a policy that gets 99% in simulation can plummet to 30% on hardware. Without mapping the sources of the gap, you optimize for the wrong place.

Key concepts: reality gap, contact dynamics, sensor noise, actuation delay, physics fidelity.

What it is: aggressively randomize sim parameters so the real world becomes “just another sample” from the training distribution.

Why it matters: is the cheapest and most robust technique for zero-shot transfer; it was what enabled robots to go from simulation to real-world manipulation.

Key concepts: visual DR, dynamics DR, automatic DR (ADR), difficulty curriculum.

What it is: instead of covering everything with noise, brings the feature distribution in sim and reality closer (GANs, adversarial, embedding alignment).

Why it matters: complements DR when the visual gap is too large to randomize.

Key concepts: domain adaptation, feature alignment, RCAN, real-in-the-loop.

What it is: measure the real robot to calibrate the simulator (masses, friction, latencies) and build a faithful digital twin.

Why it matters: reduces the gap at its source; instead of covering uncertainty, you eliminate it where possible.

Key concepts: sysID, digital twin, calibration, Isaac Sim/MuJoCo tuning.

What it is: mix cheap simulation data with a few expensive real demonstrations; simulation provides coverage, real data provides final fidelity.

Why it matters: is the dominant practical recipe—95% sim + 5% real beats either alone.

Key concepts: co-training, fine-tuning, sample efficiency, real-world adaptation.

What it is: protocols that measure performance in the real world under variation, not in the sim where you trained.

Why it matters: sim-score inflates expectations; only real A/B testing counts for deployment.

Key concepts: real-world success rate, held-out, sim2real gap metric.

3.2

🤖 Humanoids: Figure, Tesla, Unitree, GR00T

The bet on a general-purpose body for a world built by humans.

What it is: the thesis that the human form unlocks the world’s stock of tools, ladders, and environments without modifying them.

Why it matters: morphological generality + foundation models = one body, infinite tasks.

Key concepts: morphological generality, brownfield deployment, dexterity.

What it is: open foundation model for humanoids with a dual system (reasoning + action) trained on real, synthetic, and neural-trajectory data.

Why it matters: NVIDIA provides the "brain + data pipeline (Isaac/Cosmos)" adopted by several OEMs.

Key concepts: GR00T N1/N1.5, dual-system, Isaac GR00T, synthetic data.

What it is: Figure’s architecture with System 2 (slow VLM, ~7-9Hz) that reasons and System 1 (fast policy, ~200Hz) that controls dexterous hands.

Why it matters: resolves the generality × frequency trade-off; pioneered full-torso control via VLA.

Key concepts: Helix, System 1/2, latent vector, whole-upper-body.

What it is: Tesla humanoid reusing end-to-end vision and FSD compute, focusing on internal manufacturing first.

Why it matters: production scale advantage and vertically integrated teleoperation data.

Key concepts: Optimus Gen 2/3, FSD reuse, industrial scale, proprietary actuators.

What it is: low-cost Chinese humanoids (G1 starting at ~US$ 16k) that democratized research on real hardware.

Why it matters: lowers the barrier to entry; became the standard platform for locomotion/manipulation papers.

Key concepts: Unitree G1/H1, cost, accessible hardware, open SDK.

What it is: coordinate gait, balance, and arms in a single controller; RL for robust locomotion on terrain.

Why it matters: manipulating while walking is what separates a demo from real-world utility.

Key concepts: whole-body control, locomotion RL, balance, loco-manipulation.

3.3

🧠 Hybrid Architectures: LLM Planner + VLA Executor

Think slowly and act quickly in the same robot.

What it is: separate high-level planning (slow, abstract) from reactive control (fast, continuous).

Why it matters: no single network is good at both long-horizon reasoning and a 200Hz control loop.

Key concepts: hierarchy, symbolic planning, reactive policy.

What it is: A slow VLM "thinks" and emits a latent vector; a fast policy consumes it to generate high-frequency actions.

Why it matters: is the convergent architecture of leading humanoids in 2026.

Key concepts: dual-system, latent conditioning, async inference.

What it is: use an LLM to break a long instruction into executable subtasks, optionally as code.

Why it matters: unlocks long-horizon tasks without training a giant monolithic policy.

Key concepts: SayCan, Code-as-Policies, task decomposition, prompting.

What it is: connect the planner’s language to skills the robot can actually execute in its current state.

Why it matters: avoids beautiful but impossible plans; the "can" in SayCan.

Key concepts: affordance, grounding, value function, feasibility.

What it is: close the loop — detect failure, update memory, and replan instead of blindly continuing.

Why it matters: robustness comes from recovery, not perfect execution.

Key concepts: closed-loop, inner monologue, replanning, memory.

What it is: chain dozens of skills for minutes while maintaining coherence and robustness.

Why it matters: is the current bottleneck—long tasks still accumulate failures.

Key concepts: long horizon, skill composition, robustness, drift.

3.4

🏭 Applications: Manufacturing, Logistics, Healthcare, Agriculture

Where general-purpose robotics is already creating value—and where it still falls short.

What it is: flexible assembly, visual inspection, and collaborative cobots with ROI measured in payback cycles.

Why it matters: is the most mature market; VLA reduces the cost of reprogramming for each SKU.

Key concepts: cobot, payback, flexibility, inspection.

What it is: separation and packaging in warehouses, the most commercialized use case for AI-powered manipulation.

Why it matters: high volume, a semi-structured environment, and clear ROI: today’s “killer app.”

Key concepts: bin picking, Covariant RFM, Symbotic, Amazon, fulfillment.

What it is: assisted surgery, tissue manipulation and care, with extremely high safety requirements.

Why it matters: high margins, but enormous regulatory and reliability barriers.

Key concepts: assisted surgery, force control, regulation, assistance.

What it is: harvest delicate fruit, map plants, and operate in a fully unstructured environment.

Why it matters: labor shortages create demand; natural variability is the challenge.

Key concepts: selective harvesting, phenotyping, outdoor, variability.

What it is: cooking, cleaning, and tidying the house — the most variable environment and the least tolerant of social errors.

Why it matters: largest potential market, but the last to close technically.

Key concepts: open-world, domestic, generalization, long tail.

What it is: safety, total cost, reliability, and integration with legacy systems.

Why it matters: technology is rarely the bottleneck; integration and trust are.

Key concepts: TCO, MTBF, safety, integration, change management.

3.6

🚀 2026-2030 frontier: Scaling Laws, World Models, Certification

What's still uncertain—and what changes when it's resolved.

What it is: the billion-dollar question—is performance scaling predictably with data and compute, as it does for LLMs?

Why it matters: if yes, the race becomes one of data collection; if not, the architecture still matters.

Key concepts: scaling laws, data×compute, uncertainty, embodiment.

What it is: models that learn the world's dynamics to imagine futures, generate data, and plan (Genie, NVIDIA Cosmos).

Why it matters: cheap neural simulation could break the data bottleneck.

Key concepts: world model, Genie, Cosmos, neural sim, planning.

What it is: robots collecting their own data via RL at scale and autonomous data collection.

Why it matters: the flywheel that can make continuous improvement inexpensive.

Key concepts: self-improvement, RL at scale, autonomy, flywheel.

What it is: one brain, many bodies — fleets that learn together and share improvements.

Why it matters: each robot improves them all; compounding scaling advantage.

Key concepts: fleet learning, cross-embodiment, transfer, OTA.

What it is: ensure learned robots can be certified — ISO safety standards, fail-safe behavior, physical alignment.

Why it matters: without certification, there is no large-scale deployment around humans.

Key concepts: ISO 10218/15066, fail-safe, warranties, physical alignment.

What it is: projections of the impact of general-purpose robotics on labor, productivity, and economic structure.

Why it matters: career and business decisions depend on which scenario you believe.

Key concepts: 2030 scenarios, productivity, displacement, transition.
